Decoration & Environment

By SiripJuara

June 05, 2026 | 12.33 WITA

A good environment directly impacts the health and comfort of betta fish. The container must be spacious enough for the fish to move freely, with decorations that are not excessive. The main focus should always be on safety and water quality — not just appearance.

  • Prioritize Safety: use smooth, non-sharp, and non-toxic decorations
  • Maintain Swimming Space: don't let decorations make the container cramped
  • Use Aquatic Plants: live or soft artificial plants for a sense of security
  • Avoid Excessive Decorations: they cause waste buildup
  • Choose Easy-to-Clean Items: to keep water quality maintained
  • Set Up Proper Lighting: not too bright so the fish doesn't get stressed
  • Mind the Placement: avoid noisy areas, vibrations, or extreme temperature exposure

A well-arranged environment helps betta fish feel comfortable and reduces stress. The elements used can also function as shelter and resting areas. Additionally, use safe and non-sharp decorations so they don't damage the fish's fins.

1. Aquatic Plants

Plants serve as resting areas for the fish. They also help create a more natural atmosphere and make the fish feel safer. The plants used can be live or artificial, but it's best to choose soft materials so they don't damage the betta's fins.

2. Rocks and Gravel

Used as substrate or the base of the container, rocks and gravel help create a more natural environment. Choose smooth, non-sharp rocks so they don't harm the fish.

3. Hiding Spots

Small caves or certain ornaments can serve as hiding spots for the fish. These areas help betta fish feel safer, especially when they are adapting to a new environment.

4. Avoid Sharp Decorations

Always use fish-safe decorations with smooth, non-sharp surfaces. Rough decorations can damage the betta's delicate fins and lead to injury or infection.

5. Adequate Lighting

Use moderate lighting — not too bright. Adequate light helps the fish stay active, while overly strong light can easily stress the fish and make it uncomfortable. Aim for 8–12 hours of light per day with a consistent schedule.
